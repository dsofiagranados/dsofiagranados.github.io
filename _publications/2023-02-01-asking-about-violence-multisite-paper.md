Surveys about political violence inform scholarship and policy, but researchers and ethics committees worry that asking about violence may harm participants. There is little causal evidence on the effects of asking. We combined a systematic review with three randomized field experiments in Colombia, Mexico, and Nigeria, randomizing whether more than 9,000 participants were asked about political violence and measuring multiple dimensions of their welfare and research experiences immediately and, in two sites, several weeks later. Asking consistently made participants' immediate experience more negative, although absolute evaluations of participation remained generally favorable. Asking did not increase regret, and most adverse effects had faded at follow-up. Effects on trauma-related reactions, mental health symptoms, and perceived benefits varied across sites, and some effects depended on question design. Participant welfare should therefore not be conceptualized as a continuum from harm to benefit. We propose an evidence-informed process for deciding whether and how to ask about violence.
