Bridging political science and developmental psychology, I study how the timing and nature of exposure to violence shape people's long-term attitudes toward peace and the reintegration of ex-combatants, drawing on fieldwork in Colombia and Latin America.

Dissertation

My dissertation develops a theory of the heterogeneous, long-term effects of violence and asks how they can inform peace-building and early-childhood policy in post-conflict settings. It comprises three projects:

Developmental timing of exposure When

Does the developmental stage at which a person first experiences violence shape their long-term attitudes toward the reintegration of ex-combatants? Drawing on the idea of sensitive periods, the project distinguishes early childhood, middle childhood, and adolescence, using Asking About Violence and Violence Against Children Survey data.

Armed-group governance and normalization How

Do people who lived under armed-group governance hold different political attitudes than those who experienced only armed-group presence, or neither? Using subnational variation across Colombian municipalities, the project develops normalization as the mechanism linking sustained exposure to political attitudes and behavior.

Fostering empathy in conflict-affected children Intervention

Can a Theory of Mind intervention increase empathy toward out-groups among displaced and victimized children and adolescents? The project develops and evaluates an intervention to foster peace-building attitudes early in the life course.
